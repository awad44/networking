Slide 5

Scheduling disciplines

1

FIFO

Single queue, first come first served. Simple, no isolation: one aggressive flow hurts everyone.

2

Strict priority (PQ)

Always serve the highest non-empty queue. Lowest delay for top class but can starve lower classes.

3

Round robin / WRR

Cycle through queues, serving one (or w) packets each. Unfair with variable packet sizes.

4

Fair queueing / WFQ

Emulates bit-by-bit round robin (GPS) via virtual finish times; per-flow or per-class weights.

5

Deficit round robin

Each queue gets a quantum of bytes per round plus carry-over deficit. O(1), byte-fair, used in Linux and switches.

6

CBWFQ + LLQ

Class-based WFQ with a strict-priority low-latency queue for voice, policed to prevent starvation.

Slide 6

Scheduling disciplines

EXPLAINED

When several queues have packets waiting for the same output link, a scheduler decides which packet goes next. FIFO has a single queue — simple, but a burst from one flow delays everyone behind it.
Strict priority always empties higher-priority queues first: ideal for latency, but if the top queue never empties, lower queues starve. Round robin visits each queue in turn; weighted round robin serves several packets from heavier queues. Both count packets, so a queue with large packets gets more bytes than one with small packets.
Fair queueing and WFQ fix this by scheduling according to bytes, approximating an ideal fluid system. DRR achieves byte fairness cheaply with a per-queue credit. Real equipment typically uses LLQ + CBWFQ: one strict-priority queue for voice, protected by a policer so it cannot starve the others, plus weighted queues for other classes.

EXAMPLE

Two queues in round robin: queue A has 1500-byte packets, queue B has 64-byte packets. Each gets one packet per turn, so A receives about 23 times more bandwidth than B.

REMEMBER

PQ = lowest delay but risk of starvation. WFQ/DRR = fair bandwidth sharing. LLQ = PQ with a policer + weighted queues.

Weighted Fair Queueing (WFQ)

Ideal model — GPS: serve all backlogged flows simultaneously in proportion to weights wᵢ
Guarantee: flow i gets at least C · wᵢ / Σw when backlogged
Packet approximation: compute a virtual finish time Fᵢ = max(Fᵢ_prev, V(t)) + Lᵢ / wᵢ
Transmit the packet with the smallest finish time
Parekh–Gallager: a token-bucket-shaped flow in WFQ has a bounded worst-case delay ≈ b / rᵢ + Lmax/rᵢ + Lmax/C per hop

W

Why it matters

WFQ is the theoretical foundation of guaranteed service: combining a token-bucket arrival curve with a weighted rate gives a hard delay bound. This is exactly what IntServ Guaranteed Service relies on.

Slide 8

Weighted Fair Queueing (WFQ)

EXPLAINED

The ideal scheduler, called Generalized Processor Sharing (GPS), serves all non-empty queues at the same time, each at a share proportional to its weight. Real links send one packet at a time, so WFQ simulates GPS: it computes when each packet would have finished in the ideal system (its virtual finish time) and sends the packet with the earliest finish time.
A packet’s finish time grows with its length divided by its weight, so large packets and low-weight queues are naturally served later. As a result, every backlogged flow receives at least C × wᵢ / Σw of the capacity, no matter how aggressive other flows are.
Parekh and Gallager proved that if a flow is limited by a token bucket (r, b) and WFQ gives it a rate of at least r, its delay at each hop is bounded. This mathematical guarantee is the basis of IntServ’s Guaranteed Service.

EXAMPLE

Weights 3 and 1 on a 10 Mb/s link: if both queues are busy, they receive 7.5 and 2.5 Mb/s. If queue 2 goes idle, queue 1 can use all 10 Mb/s.

REMEMBER

WFQ = weighted, byte-fair sharing with a guaranteed minimum rate and bounded delay for shaped flows.

Slide 10

Deficit Round Robin example (quantum = 500 B)

EXPLAINED

DRR keeps a deficit counter for each queue. Every round, each non-empty queue receives a quantum of credit (here 500 bytes). The queue may send packets as long as the packet at its head is no larger than its current credit; the credit is reduced by each packet sent.
Unused credit is carried over to the next round, so a queue with a big packet eventually accumulates enough to send it. When a queue becomes empty, its deficit is reset to zero, so an idle queue cannot save up credit and burst later.
In the example, queue A’s small packets go out in rounds 1 and 2, while queue B must wait until round 3 to accumulate 1500 bytes of credit for its 1200-byte packet. Over many rounds both queues send the same number of bytes, regardless of packet size, with only O(1) work per packet.

EXAMPLE

Give queue A a quantum of 1000 and B 500: A now receives twice the bandwidth of B — this is how weights are expressed in DRR.

REMEMBER

DRR = round robin with byte credits. Simple, fast and fair. Linux and many switches use it.

Slide 13

Tail drop vs RED / WRED

EXPLAINED

With tail drop, packets are discarded only when the queue is completely full. Because many TCP flows share the queue, they all experience losses at the same moment, all halve their windows together, and then all grow again together. This global synchronisation makes the link alternate between overload and under-use, and keeps the queue (and delay) high.
RED (Random Early Detection) watches the average queue length. Below a minimum threshold it never drops; above a maximum threshold it drops everything; in between it drops randomly with a probability that rises linearly. A few flows are slowed early, before the queue overflows, so flows back off at different times.
WRED uses different thresholds for different markings. Packets with high drop precedence (for example AF13) start being dropped at a lower queue length than packets with low drop precedence (AF11). With ECN, RED can mark packets instead of dropping them.

EXAMPLE

WRED profile: AF11 dropped from 30–40 packets, AF12 from 25–40, AF13 from 20–40. As the queue grows, AF13 traffic is sacrificed first, protecting in-contract AF11 traffic.

REMEMBER

Tail drop synchronises TCP flows. RED/WRED drop early and selectively to keep queues short and stable.

15

Slide 16

IntServ architecture components

EXPLAINED

Integrated Services aims to give individual flows guaranteed treatment end to end, similar to a telephone circuit. A flow describes its traffic with a TSpec (token-bucket rate and burst, peak rate, packet sizes) and requests service with an RSpec (the rate it wants reserved and how much delay slack it can accept).
A signalling protocol, RSVP, carries these descriptions to every router on the path. Each router runs admission control: if it cannot honour the new request without breaking existing ones, it refuses.
If accepted, the router installs a classifier entry that recognises the flow’s packets by their 5-tuple and configures its scheduler (typically WFQ) to give the flow the reserved rate. Every router therefore keeps state for every reserved flow.

EXAMPLE

A video-conference flow requests r = 2 Mb/s, b = 20 KB. Each router checks spare capacity on the outgoing link, reserves 2 Mb/s in its WFQ scheduler and adds a 5-tuple filter.

REMEMBER

IntServ = describe the flow (TSpec), request service (RSpec), signal (RSVP), admit, classify and schedule — per flow, per router.

17

Slide 18

IntServ service classes

EXPLAINED

Guaranteed Service gives a firm, mathematically provable upper bound on queueing delay and no queueing loss, provided the flow respects its token bucket. It is built on WFQ and the Parekh–Gallager bound, and suits applications that cannot adapt, such as circuit emulation.
Controlled-Load Service makes a weaker promise: the flow will experience service close to that of an unloaded network — low loss and low delay — but no specific numbers are guaranteed. Admission control ensures the network is not overbooked.
Traffic without a reservation simply receives best effort. In practice controlled-load is much easier to provide, because it does not require precise worst-case calculations at every hop.

EXAMPLE

An adaptive video player that can adjust its buffer is happy with controlled load. A legacy industrial control system that fails if delay exceeds 20 ms needs guaranteed service.

REMEMBER

Guaranteed = hard numeric bound. Controlled-load = “like an empty network”. Others = best effort.

Slide 20

RSVP signalling (RFC 2205)

EXPLAINED

RSVP reserves resources along the path that packets will actually follow. The sender first sends a PATH message towards the receiver. Each router records the previous hop (path state) and can add information about its capabilities (ADSpec).
The receiver then answers with a RESV message containing the FlowSpec it wants. RESV travels hop by hop back along the recorded reverse path. Each router performs admission control and, if successful, reserves the resources and forwards RESV upstream. Reservations are receiver-initiated because receivers know what quality they need — important for multicast, where different receivers may want different quality.
RSVP uses soft state: PATH and RESV are refreshed periodically (about every 30 s). If refreshes stop — because a host crashed or the route changed — the reservation times out automatically.

EXAMPLE

If the routing changes, new PATH refreshes follow the new route and set up state there, while the old reservations simply expire. No explicit tear-down is required (though PathTear/ResvTear exist).

REMEMBER

PATH downstream sets up the path; RESV upstream makes the reservation; periodic refreshes keep it alive.

Slide 21

Why IntServ did not scale

1

Per-flow state

A core router may carry millions of flows — impossible to keep state, classifiers and refreshes for each.

2

Signalling overhead

Periodic PATH/RESV refresh for every flow on every hop consumes CPU and bandwidth.

3

End-to-end deployment

Every router and every domain must support it — no incentive for partial deployment.

4

Where it lives on

RSVP-TE for MPLS traffic engineering (per-LSP, not per-flow) and some enterprise video systems.

21

Slide 22

Why IntServ did not scale

EXPLAINED

IntServ works well in small networks, but the Internet core carries millions of simultaneous flows. Keeping a reservation, classifier entry and scheduler state for each flow on every core router is too expensive in memory and processing.
Soft state makes it worse: every reservation must be refreshed every 30 seconds on every hop, creating a constant stream of signalling messages. And the benefit only appears if every router and every provider on the path supports IntServ — there is little reward for deploying it alone.
RSVP did not disappear: RSVP-TE is widely used to reserve bandwidth for MPLS traffic-engineering tunnels. There it signals a few thousand aggregate tunnels instead of millions of individual flows, which scales well. For general QoS, the industry moved to DiffServ, the topic of the next lecture.

EXAMPLE

A core router with 2 million flows and 30 s refreshes would process about 130 000 RSVP messages per second just to maintain existing reservations.

REMEMBER

Per-flow state does not scale to the core. Aggregate (per-class) treatment does — that is DiffServ.

Slide 25

Model answers

A1

Round robin sends one packet per queue per turn, so queues with bigger packets get more bytes. DRR gives each queue a byte credit (quantum) per round and carries over unused credit, so bytes, not packets, are shared fairly.

A2

With tail drop many TCP flows lose packets at the same moment and back off and ramp up together, wasting capacity. RED drops randomly before the queue is full, so different flows back off at different times.

A3

PATH installs path state (previous hop) so RESV can follow the exact reverse path. Receivers know the quality they need (and in multicast may differ), so they request the reservation.

A4

Per-flow state on every core router (memory, classification, scheduling) and constant soft-state refresh signalling; also it requires deployment on every router and domain along the path.

Key takeaways

Schedulers trade isolation, fairness and latency: PQ for voice, WFQ/DRR for fair sharing

RED/WRED drop early and probabilistically to avoid global synchronisation

IntServ offers per-flow Guaranteed and Controlled-Load service using RSVP

RSVP is receiver-initiated, soft-state and follows the routing table

Per-flow state does not scale — motivating DiffServ
