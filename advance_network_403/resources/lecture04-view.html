The case for QoS

IP is best effort: every packet treated equally, no guarantees on delay or loss
Converged networks carry voice, video, interactive and bulk traffic on the same links
Congestion is local and transient: a 1 Gb/s access link feeding a 100 Mb/s WAN link will queue
Over-provisioning helps but is expensive and fails at bottlenecks (WAN, wireless, uplinks)
QoS = managed unfairness: decide who suffers when resources are scarce

Q

QoS does not create bandwidth

QoS only redistributes delay, jitter and loss among traffic classes during congestion. If there is no congestion, QoS has nothing to do.

Slide 6

The case for QoS

EXPLAINED

IP networks are best effort: routers forward packets in the order they arrive and drop them when queues are full, without knowing what the packets carry. This is simple and fair, but it treats a voice packet that must arrive within 150 ms exactly like a backup packet that could wait minutes.
Today a single link often carries voice, video conferencing, business applications, web browsing and large backups together. Whenever the offered traffic exceeds the link speed — even for a few milliseconds — a queue builds. Without QoS, the voice packet waits behind backup packets and the call becomes choppy.
Quality of Service is the set of mechanisms that decide who waits and who is dropped when there is congestion. It cannot make a link faster; it only redistributes delay and loss so that the most sensitive traffic is protected.

EXAMPLE

A branch office has a 1 Gb/s LAN but a 50 Mb/s WAN link. When a user starts a large download, the WAN queue fills and the CEO’s video call freezes. QoS lets the router send call packets first.

REMEMBER

QoS matters only at congestion points (WAN links, uplinks, Wi-Fi). It is “managed unfairness”.

Slide 7

QoS metrics

B

Bandwidth

Throughput available to a flow or class (b/s). Guaranteed minimum or capped maximum.

D

Delay (latency)

One-way end-to-end: propagation + transmission + processing + queueing. Only queueing is controllable by QoS.

J

Jitter

Variation in delay between packets of a flow (IPDV, RFC 3393). Absorbed by play-out buffers at the cost of extra delay.

L

Loss

Fraction of packets dropped, mostly from queue overflow or policing. Also burst-loss patterns matter.

A

Availability

Fraction of time the service meets its targets — usually written into an SLA (e.g. 99.99%).

O

Ordering

Reordering confuses TCP (spurious dupACKs). QoS must avoid splitting a flow across queues.

7

Slide 8

QoS metrics

EXPLAINED

QoS is described with a small set of measurable quantities. Bandwidth is how much data per second a class can send. Delay is the time from sender to receiver; it is the sum of transmission time (pushing bits onto the wire), propagation time (distance), processing time and queueing time. Only queueing time can be controlled by QoS.
Jitter is the variation in delay between consecutive packets. Real-time applications use a play-out buffer to smooth it out, but a bigger buffer means more delay. Loss is the fraction of packets dropped, usually because a queue was full or a policer discarded excess traffic.
In service contracts these are combined with availability (the percentage of time targets are met). Also important is that packets of the same flow stay in order: splitting a TCP flow across queues can reorder packets and trigger false retransmissions.

EXAMPLE

Two VoIP packets sent 20 ms apart arrive 20 ms and 45 ms after sending: delay is 20 and 45 ms, jitter is 25 ms. The receiver’s jitter buffer must hold at least 25 ms of audio.

REMEMBER

Bandwidth, delay, jitter, loss (plus availability). QoS controls queueing — the variable part of delay.

Slide 10

Application requirements

EXPLAINED

Applications differ hugely in what they can tolerate. Interactive voice needs a one-way delay below about 150 ms (ITU-T G.114), low jitter and less than about 1% loss, but very little bandwidth per call. Interactive video has similar delay targets but needs far more bandwidth.
Streaming video is buffered several seconds ahead, so it tolerates delay and jitter well but needs sustained throughput. Interactive data (web apps, ERP) needs reasonable response times. Bulk transfers such as backups care only about total throughput and can absorb delay and loss because TCP recovers.
A good QoS design starts by listing the organisation’s applications and placing each in one of these classes. The class determines the queue, the bandwidth guarantee and the drop behaviour it will receive.

EXAMPLE

A Netflix stream can pause for a second without the user noticing (buffer). A one-second pause in a phone call is unacceptable.

REMEMBER

Classify applications by sensitivity to delay, jitter and loss — not by how “important” the user thinks they are.

Per-hop QoS pipeline

12

Slide 13

Per-hop QoS pipeline

EXPLAINED

Inside a router or switch, QoS is a pipeline of tools. Classification identifies which class a packet belongs to — by IP addresses, ports, protocol, existing markings or even application signatures. Marking writes the class into the packet header so later devices do not have to classify again.
Policing and shaping enforce rate limits defined in a contract. Queueing and scheduling place each class into its own queue and decide which queue to serve next. Dropping / AQM decides which packets to discard when a queue grows too long.
Expensive classification is done once at the network edge, where traffic enters and flow counts are small. Core routers only read the marking and apply the corresponding queue and drop policy, which keeps them fast and simple.

EXAMPLE

An access switch sees traffic from an IP phone on UDP ports 16384–32767, marks it EF. Every router along the path then puts EF packets into its priority queue without inspecting ports again.

REMEMBER

Classify and mark at the edge; queue, schedule and drop according to the mark everywhere else.

Slide 15

Where QoS markings live

EXPLAINED

Markings can be stored at different layers. In Ethernet, an 802.1Q VLAN tag contains a 3-bit Priority Code Point (CoS 0–7). It only exists on the local Layer-2 segment: when a router forwards the packet onto another link, the Ethernet header (and the marking) is replaced.
In IP, the DSCP field (6 bits) in the IPv4 ToS byte or IPv6 Traffic Class travels end-to-end with the packet, so it is the main marking used across a network. MPLS networks copy the class into the 3-bit Traffic Class field of each label, and Wi-Fi maps it to four WMM access categories that change how aggressively a station contends for the air.
Because endpoints could mark their own traffic as high priority to get better service, networks define a trust boundary: markings from trusted devices (IP phones, servers) are kept, all others are re-marked at the access edge.

EXAMPLE

A laptop marks all its traffic EF to “jump the queue”. The access switch does not trust laptops, so it re-marks everything from that port to DSCP 0 unless it matches a known video-conferencing application.

REMEMBER

DSCP is the end-to-end marking; CoS, MPLS TC and WMM are local mappings of it. Never trust markings blindly.

Slide 17

Leaky bucket vs token bucket

Leaky bucket

Packets enter a bucket (queue) of size B
Leave at a constant rate r
Overflow is dropped
Output is perfectly smooth — no bursts at all
Good for strict rate enforcement (ATM GCRA)

Token bucket

Tokens added at rate r up to depth b
A packet of L bytes needs L tokens
Saved tokens allow bursts up to b
Over any interval t: data ≤ r·t + b
Used by policers and shapers everywhere

IN403  ·  Lecture 4  ·  QoS Fundamentals & Traffic Shaping

17

Slide 18

Leaky bucket vs token bucket

EXPLAINED

Both algorithms describe and enforce a traffic rate. In a leaky bucket, packets are poured into a bucket with a hole: they leave at a perfectly constant rate r. If too many arrive, the bucket overflows and packets are dropped. Output can never be bursty.
In a token bucket, the bucket holds tokens, not packets. Tokens are added at rate r up to a maximum depth b. To send a packet of L bytes, L tokens must be removed. If the source was quiet, tokens accumulate, so it can later send a burst of up to b bytes at full line rate.
The token bucket therefore describes real traffic better: over any interval of length t, a conforming source sends at most r·t + b bytes. This simple formula is used in contracts, in policers, in shapers and in IntServ reservations.

EXAMPLE

r = 1 Mb/s, b = 100 KB. After 10 s of silence the bucket is full, so the source can immediately send 100 KB at line speed, then continue at 1 Mb/s.

REMEMBER

Leaky bucket = smooth constant output. Token bucket = average rate r with bursts up to b.

Slide 20

Token bucket: worked example

EXPLAINED

Consider a contract with CIR (committed information rate) r = 2 Mb/s and CBS (committed burst size) b = 250 KB = 2 Mb, on an access line of R = 10 Mb/s. How long can the customer send at the full line rate?
While sending at 10 Mb/s, tokens are consumed at 10 Mb/s but refilled at 2 Mb/s, so the bucket empties at a net rate of R − r = 8 Mb/s. A full bucket of 2 Mb lasts 2 / 8 = 0.25 s. During that quarter-second the customer sends 2.5 Mb: 2 Mb from the saved tokens and 0.5 Mb from new tokens.
Over any longer period the upper bound is r·t + b. In 10 seconds the customer may send at most 20 Mb + 2 Mb = 22 Mb. Anything beyond is non-conforming and will be dropped (policing) or delayed (shaping).

EXAMPLE

Same contract over 1 minute: max 2 × 60 + 2 = 122 Mb ≈ 15.25 MB. Average 2 Mb/s plus a single 2 Mb burst allowance.

REMEMBER

Burst duration at peak = b / (R − r). Maximum data in time t = r·t + b.

Policing vs shaping

Policing

Excess traffic is dropped or re-marked
No buffering ⇒ no added delay
Causes TCP loss and saw-tooth throughput
Applied inbound or outbound
Typical at provider ingress (enforce the contract)

Shaping

Excess traffic is queued and delayed
Smooth output, fewer drops
Adds delay and needs buffers
Applied outbound only
Typical at customer egress (conform to the contract)

21

Slide 22

Policing vs shaping

EXPLAINED

Both tools compare traffic against a token bucket; they differ in what they do with excess packets. A policer drops them (or re-marks them to a lower class) immediately. It adds no delay and needs no buffer, but drops cause TCP to cut its window, so TCP through a policer shows a saw-tooth and often achieves well below the policed rate.
A shaper holds excess packets in a queue and releases them when tokens are available. The output is smooth and drops are rare, but packets are delayed and the device needs buffer memory. Shaping is only possible on output, because you cannot delay packets that have already arrived on an input.
In a typical design, the service provider polices at its ingress to enforce the contract, and the customer shapes at its egress to stay within that contract, so the provider’s policer never has to drop anything.

EXAMPLE

A customer buys 20 Mb/s on a 100 Mb/s port. Without shaping, bursts at 100 Mb/s are dropped by the provider’s policer. With a 20 Mb/s shaper on the customer router, bursts are queued for a few ms instead of lost.

REMEMBER

Policing: drop excess, no delay. Shaping: queue excess, add delay. Provider polices, customer shapes.

Policer colour models

1

Single-rate two-colour

One token bucket (CIR, CBS). Conform ⇒ transmit (green); exceed ⇒ drop (red).

2

srTCM (RFC 2697)

Single rate, two buckets: CBS and EBS. Green / yellow (re-mark to higher drop precedence) / red.

3

trTCM (RFC 2698)

Two rates: CIR and PIR, each with its own bucket. Traffic above PIR is red, between CIR and PIR is yellow.

23

Slide 24

Policer colour models

EXPLAINED

A simple policer has two outcomes: conform (send) or exceed (drop). Standards define richer “colour” models with three outcomes, so traffic slightly over contract can be forwarded with a lower priority instead of dropped.
The single-rate three-colour marker (srTCM) uses one rate (CIR) and two bucket sizes: packets within the committed burst are green, those using the extra burst (EBS) are yellow, and the rest are red. The two-rate three-colour marker (trTCM) uses two rates: up to CIR is green, between CIR and the peak rate (PIR) is yellow, above PIR is red.
Green packets are forwarded normally, yellow packets are forwarded but re-marked with a higher drop precedence (for example from AF11 to AF12), and red packets are dropped. Under congestion, yellow traffic is discarded first.

EXAMPLE

Contract CIR 10 Mb/s, PIR 20 Mb/s. A customer sending 15 Mb/s has 10 Mb/s green and 5 Mb/s yellow: all is delivered when the network is quiet, but the yellow part is dropped first at busy times.

REMEMBER

Green = within contract, yellow = excess but allowed if capacity exists, red = dropped.

Shaping and policing with Linux tc

# Shape egress to 10 Mbit/s with Token Bucket Filter
tc qdisc add dev eth0 root tbf \
    rate 10mbit burst 32kbit latency 50ms

# Hierarchical shaping with HTB
tc qdisc add dev eth0 root handle 1: htb default 20
tc class add dev eth0 parent 1: classid 1:1 htb rate 100mbit
tc class add dev eth0 parent 1:1 classid 1:10 htb rate 20mbit ceil 100mbit prio 0
tc class add dev eth0 parent 1:1 classid 1:20 htb rate 80mbit ceil 100mbit prio 1

# Police ingress traffic to 5 Mbit/s
tc qdisc add dev eth0 ingress
tc filter add dev eth0 parent ffff: protocol ip u32 \
    match u32 0 0 police rate 5mbit burst 64k drop

tbf: simple single-rate shaper
htb: guaranteed rate + borrowable ceil per class

police action drops excess with no queue

Slide 26

Shaping and policing with Linux tc

EXPLAINED

Linux implements QoS with the tc (traffic control) command. A qdisc (queueing discipline) is attached to an interface. tbf (token bucket filter) is the simplest shaper: rate is r, burst is b, and latency limits how long a packet may wait in the queue before being dropped.
HTB (hierarchical token bucket) builds a tree of classes. Each class has a rate (guaranteed bandwidth) and a ceil (maximum it may reach by borrowing unused bandwidth from its parent). Filters assign packets to classes, for example by DSCP or port.
Policing uses the police action, usually on the special ingress qdisc for incoming traffic. It has no queue: packets above the rate are dropped immediately. The command tc -s qdisc show prints counters for sent, dropped and over-limit packets, which is how you verify a policy works.

EXAMPLE

An HTB class with rate 20mbit ceil 100mbit is guaranteed 20 Mb/s and may use up to 100 Mb/s when other classes are idle.

REMEMBER

tbf = simple shaper, htb = hierarchical shaping with borrowing, police = drop excess on ingress. Lab 3 uses all three.

Slide 29

Model answers

A1

Delay and jitter (voice tolerates little of either). QoS controls only queueing delay; propagation and transmission delay are fixed by distance and link speed.

A2

Classification can be complex (ports, applications) and needs per-flow inspection; doing it once at the edge, where traffic volume per device is small, keeps core routers fast and allows them to act only on the DSCP.

A3

r·t + b = 1 Mb/s × 2 s + 50 KB = 2 Mb + 0.4 Mb = 2.4 Mb (= 300 KB).

A4

Police at a provider’s ingress to enforce a contract (drop excess, no delay). Shape at a customer’s egress to stay within the contract and avoid drops (queue excess, adds delay).

Key takeaways

QoS manages delay, jitter, loss and bandwidth only during congestion

Different applications need very different service levels (voice ≠ backup)

The pipeline is classify → mark → police/shape → queue/schedule → drop

A token bucket (r, b) bounds traffic to r·t + b over any interval t

Policing drops excess immediately; shaping buffers it and adds delay
